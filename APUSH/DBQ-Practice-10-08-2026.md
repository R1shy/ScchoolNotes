### Prompt: Evaluate the extent of change in ideas about American independence from 1763 to 1783

The seeds of independence were planted with the founding of the House of Burgesses in 1619 which was the first assembly of colonists in the colonies. From 1763 to 1783 attitude towards the idea of American independence by the colonists shifted from unthinkable and not even an option to necessary for the continued existence of the colonies.

The opinions of the colonists did not change overnight, it was over many years that the colonists became more in support of independence from the crown. In document 2 the House of Burgesses does declare that they should be the authority on tax law and collection but they vehemently declare their loyalty and love for the king, this intensity of this declaration of loyalty could be attributed to the audience which would be the King or his court. The tide started to change by 1772 which was 3 years after the declaration by the House of Burgesses, in document 3 Samuel Adams argues that the natural liberty of a man is to be free, which is seemingly an indirect jab against the rule of the crown over the colonies, his purpose here was to express support for independence while keeping the mention hidden enough so that his audience of possible loyalists would not notice. Another 3 years later in 1775 some people still weren't convinced that revolution was the way to go in document 4 a group of quakers argue that because the king was divinely appointed they cannot in good faith support a revolution against him, the context for this is that the Quakers are pacifists so they are hoping for a peaceful and non-violent end to the issues between the colonists and the crown; But by June of that same year hostilities had reached a boiling point and it seemed there was no way for a peaceful end, in document 5 Janet Schaw talks about how the colonists have started raiding plantations and forcing the owners to either join their sides or have their crops destroyed, animals killed, and enslaved people stolen, Schaw's point of view does not necessarily support one side or the other she simply states the facts of what she has been witnessing, which makes her relatively neutral.
